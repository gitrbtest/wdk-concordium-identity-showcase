What this story shows

Checking that a private wallet belongs to the customer

Maya wants to withdraw CCD to her own wallet. Before an exchange sends money to a customer's own (private) wallet, rules for crypto firms say it must check that the wallet really belongs to that customer.

Today, on most chains

  • The wallet's address says nothing about who owns it.
  • The customer sends a small test payment from the wallet (a fee, and waiting), or uploads screenshots, or signs a message with tools most customers don't have.
  • A person at the exchange reviews it before releasing the money: hours, sometimes days.

The exchange learns that someone controls the address, not who.

With a Concordium identity in the wallet

  • Every Concordium account is opened from an identity that an identity provider has checked.
  • The exchange shows a code; the customer approves one request in their wallet.
  • The wallet proves the account is theirs and shows their name and ID number, signed by the identity provider. Nothing else about them is shared.
  • The exchange checks it against the chain in seconds, and pays out.

Proof of control and of who, with no test payment and no manual review.

    Kept simple on purpose

    A happy-path demo: what a real exchange would add

    This story shows the idea, so it leaves out things a real system needs. We know they matter; they are left out only to keep the demo short and clear.

    • The customer record is made-up test data; a real exchange has its own KYC records, keeps them up to date, and re-checks customers over time.
    • One check per withdrawal: a real exchange would remember a verified wallet (and when it was checked), so regular withdrawals need no new proof each time.
    • Other checks still apply: sanctions and fraud screening, limits, and the details exchanges share with each other under the travel rule.
    • The recording fee for each question is paid from a shared test account; a real exchange pays it from its own account or a partner's.
    • The payout comes from a shared test account, up to 50 CCD; a real exchange pays from its own wallets, with its own limits and approvals.
    • The wallet connection is a small relay on this computer; a real exchange uses WalletConnect or the wallet's browser interface.
    • Records: nothing is stored after the demo; a real exchange keeps each check as evidence for its auditors and regulators.
    • Errors: only a few are handled (a declined request, another person's account, a node that does not answer).
    • Companies could prove their wallet the same way with a Business ID (legal name and LEI), not shown here.

      Pledges from verified people, paid in USDT wherever it is
      
      
      Example Community Fund is raising money for a new playground. People pledge an amount; when the
        campaign closes with its goal reached, each pledge is paid in USDT.
      
      
      
        
      
          
      Today
      
          
        
            
      • Anyone can pledge under any name, including fake pledges that inflate a campaign to lure others in.
        • 
            
      • When the money arrives, often from an anonymous address on another chain, nobody can tell whether it
              came from the person who pledged.
        • 
            
      • Donors who want privacy must trust the organiser with their details.
        • 
          
      
          
      The organiser can't tell real pledges from fake ones, or who actually paid.
      
        
      
        
      
          
      With a Concordium identity in a WDK wallet
      
          
        
            
      • Each pledge is proved twice, in one approval: by Concordium ("a verified person, 18 or over") and by the
              wallet's Ethereum key ("I control the address that will pay").
        • 
            
      • Private by default: the board shows "verified donor"; a name appears only if the donor chooses.
        • 
            
      • Every pledge gets a public, timestamped fingerprint on Concordium: nothing personal, but unchangeable.
        • 
            
      • The payment is USDT on Ethereum, from the proven address, matched to the pledge.
        • 
          
      
          
      Concordium answers "who"; the payment happens wherever the money is.

        A new phone, and nothing is lost
        
      
        Maya loses her phone, or changes it, in the middle of a campaign. Everything happens in her wallet; the
        organisations simply go on recognising her.
        
      
        
        
        
          
        Today
        
          
          
            
        • A new phone often means signing in again and verifying again, service by service, sometimes with documents.
          • 
            
        • Whatever was only on the old phone is gone.
          • 
            
        • Commitments made from the old phone can be hard to pick up again.
          • 
          
        
          
        Changing phones means starting over in many places.
        
        
        
        
        
          
        With a Concordium identity in a WDK wallet
        
          
          
            
        • The seed phrase alone brings back her identity, from its identity provider, with no new verification.
          • 
            
        • Her Concordium account is found on chain, and her Ethereum address with its USDT is the same.
          • 
            
        • Her wallet asks Example Community Fund for her pledges, proving with her key that the paying address is hers.
          • 
            
        • She can still pay her pledge from the new phone, and Example Exchange still recognises her.
          • 
          
        
          
        Lose your phone, lose nothing: the seed phrase is the whole wallet.

        A happy-path demo: what a real wallet would add
        
      
        This story shows the idea, so it leaves out things a real system needs. We know they matter; they
        are left out only to keep the demo short and clear.
        
      
          
        
        • The seed phrase must be written down and kept safe offline; real wallets guide users through that backup, and never show it on screen without a warning.
          • 
        
        • App data (settings, history, contacts) is backed up separately by real wallets; here only what the seed phrase leads to comes back, plus the pledges the fund knows.
          • 
        
        • Finding your organisations: here the wallet knows to ask Example Community Fund; a real wallet would keep a list of the organisations it dealt with, or let them find you.
          • 
        
        • An expired identity would need a new verification; this one is valid until 2027.
